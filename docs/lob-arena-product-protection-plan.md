# LOB Arena product protection plan

Date: 2026-10-06

**Use a 30-day plan, with ownership and publication decisions first.** The recommended direction is to keep the existing MIT research core useful and public, while deciding which future commercial assets to keep private. This can run alongside the current research work.

| When | Action | Concrete result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Days 1–3** | Inventory LOB Arena's assets: code, scenario libraries, models, datasets, documentation, branding and domains. Record the creator/owner, licence, publication status and intended commercial use for each. | One asset register showing what you own, what others may reuse and what requires permission. |
| **Days 3–5** | Decide the boundary for future publication. Consider public replay tools and illustrative scenarios, with future specialist scenario packs, customer integrations and hosted workflows kept private. | A one-page publication policy that you check before each release. |
| **Week 1** | Arrange a focused IP/legal consultation, requesting a fixed fee and written answers. Review contributor ownership, employment obligations, challenge/submission terms, commercial data rights and future licensing options. | An ownership-gap list and the agreements or permissions needed to close it. |
| **Weeks 1–2** | Check "LOB Arena" for trademark conflicts and registrability in your intended markets. Review domain ownership, then decide where registration is worthwhile. | A documented name-clearance and filing decision. |
| **Week 2** | Put confidentiality measures around genuinely unpublished assets: private storage, limited access and suitable agreements with collaborators. Prepare an NDA for sensitive technical discussions. | A confidential-asset list, access rules and a reviewed NDA template. |
| **Weeks 2–4** | Speak with 3–5 potential buyers: surveillance vendors, brokers/exchanges or validation teams. Test one narrow offer and ask what evidence they would need to pay for a pilot. | A pilot specification, success criteria and evidence of willingness—or unwillingness—to pay. |

For the legal consultation, bring the asset register and ask these specific questions:

- Do I clearly own the existing work, including contributions from other people?
- Can the intended commercial service use its dependencies and market data under their current terms?
- What agreements should future contributors and contractors sign?
- How should future proprietary additions coexist with the MIT release?
- Is any **specific unpublished technical invention** worth a patent assessment?

Written ownership terms matter because paying someone to create work does not always transfer its IP to you. [WIPO ownership guidance](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/ip-business-moments/grow)

**Assess a possible patent before disclosing that invention publicly.** Do not postpone that decision until the end of the month: public disclosure can defeat European patent novelty. [EPO guidance](https://www.epo.org/en/new-to-patents/are-you-ready/2-1)

For confidentiality, protect information that is actually secret and commercially valuable. Access controls and confidentiality agreements support trade-secret protection; they cannot restore secrecy to published material. [WIPO trade-secret guidance](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/trade-secrets)

For the customer conversations, test this offer:

> LOB Arena helps you compare detector versions against controlled order-book scenarios and produce reproducible evidence of coverage, missed attacks and alert load.

Ask how they do this today, what costs them time, who approves purchases and what a useful pilot would demonstrate. Treat their answers as input to the commercial boundary.

Later customer-integration implementation belongs under the existing [Epic #93](https://github.com/khab40/lob-arena/issues/93), which is currently parked until [the secure demo in #91](https://github.com/khab40/lob-arena/issues/91) exits.

**The first practical task is the asset register.** It gives both you and a lawyer something concrete to assess before changing licences, publishing more commercial material or spending on registrations.
